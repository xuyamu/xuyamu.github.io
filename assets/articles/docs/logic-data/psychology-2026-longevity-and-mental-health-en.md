
**Outline**

Taking the Big Five personality model and longevity science as an entry point, this article first draws upon population-based studies to delineate the personality profiles of long-lived individuals and their offspring, systematically dissecting the underlying physiological and biochemical mechanisms through the lenses of hallmarks of aging, allostatic load, and the neuroendocrine system. Next, it systematically addresses the relationships between personality traits and psychological well-being, psychiatric disorders, autism, and attachment styles.

Building upon this theoretical foundation, and incorporating empirical data from the author's own anti-aging practice—including telomere reversal, the evolution of personality traits toward a longevity profile, and the amelioration of autistic traits—the paper breaks down the biochemical regulatory pathways through which systematic, multi-target anti-aging interventions (exemplified by partial chemical reprogramming) modulate the Big Five personality dimensions.

Finally, it envisions the expansive prospects of systematic anti-aging engineering in delaying organismal aging, fundamentally reshaping healthy personalities, and revolutionizing the paradigm of conventional psychiatric and psychological treatments.

**The Big Five Personality Traits**

Before delving into this topic, let us first examine a personality assessment model widely used in psychology—the Big Five. The Big Five delineates an individual's complex, multifaceted personality profile across five core dimensions.

Higher and lower scores across these five dimensions reflect distinct behavioral tendencies. The definition of the Big Five on Wikipedia states\[1\]:

Openness (O) measures creativity, curiosity, and willingness to entertain new ideas.

Conscientiousness (C) measures self-control, diligence, and attention to detail.

Extraversion (E) measures boldness, energy, and social interactivity.

Agreeableness (A) measures kindness, helpfulness, and willingness to cooperate.

Neuroticism (N) measures depression, irritability, and proneness to anxiety.

**Long-Lived Individuals and Disease**

Fries (1980) proposed the "compression of morbidity hypothesis," which posits that as human lifespan approaches its biological limit, chronic illness and periods of disability are compressed into the final stages of life\[2\]. Evert et al. (2003) studied 424 older adults aged 97 to 119 years (using medical history data provided by participants and/or their proxies) and tracked the age of onset for 10 common age-related, potentially fatal conditions (hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, stroke, non-skin cancer, skin cancer, osteoporosis, thyroid condition, Parkinson's disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease \[COPD\]). The study classified the morbidity experiences of centenarians into three distinct profiles:

Survivors (diagnosed before age 80): approximately 24% of males and 43% of females;

Delayers (diagnosed at or after age 80): approximately 44% of males and 42% of females;

Escapers (reached age 100 without any of the aforementioned major conditions): approximately 32% of males and 15% of females.

This study highlighted that exceptional longevity does not equate to a complete absence of disease. A substantial proportion of centenarians (particularly women) are "Survivors," indicating that a core hallmark of long-lived individuals is not merely the avoidance of disease, but also a remarkable capacity for physiological adaptation and survival resilience when living with chronic illness\[3\].

Andersen et al. (2012) analyzed the relationships among age of survival, morbidity, and disability among centenarians (ages 100–104), semi-supercentenarians (ages 105–109), and supercentenarians (ages 110–119). A prospective follow-up averaging 3 years (range: 0–13 years) was conducted on 104 supercentenarians, 430 semi-supercentenarians, 884 centenarians, 343 nonagenarians, and 436 controls. They found that older age was associated with a progressively delayed age of onset for conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, dementia, and stroke, as well as for cognitive and functional decline. The hazard ratios for these diseases progressively decreased with advancing age, and the duration of morbidity was correspondingly compressed\[4\].

**The Big Five Personality Traits and Long-Lived Individuals**

A targeted investigation into personality traits within the Georgia Centenarian Study (2006) revealed that centenarians generally exhibit low levels of neuroticism, accompanied by high extraversion, high competence, and high trust. Among centenarians, the specific combination of low neuroticism, high competence, and high extraversion occurred at a frequency significantly higher than would be expected by chance. These findings confirm that centenarians not only exhibit several distinct individual traits, but that a particular personality constellation—namely, low neuroticism, high competence, and high extraversion—is especially prominent within this cohort of exceptional survivors\[5\].

When turning our attention to the offspring of exceptionally long-lived individuals, the Big Five framework reveals similarly compelling patterns. A study by Givens et al. (2009) involving 246 participants demonstrated that male offspring of centenarians scored low on neuroticism and high on extraversion, while scoring at average levels for openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. Percentile distributions indicated that these men scored in the lowest third for neuroticism and in the highest third for extraversion. Female offspring likewise exhibited low neuroticism, high extraversion, and high agreeableness, while maintaining average scores in openness and conscientiousness. Specifically, women scored in the bottom third for neuroticism and in the top third for both extraversion and agreeableness\[6\].

In the larger-scale Long Life Family Study, Andersen et al. (2013) examined 4,937 participants with a mean age of 70 years. Their findings reaffirmed this phenomenon: offspring of long-lived families characteristically demonstrated the signature traits of low neuroticism and high extraversion\[7\].

One explanation for the consistently low neuroticism observed among long-lived individuals may be that elevated neuroticism increases mortality risk. Deason et al. (2025) found that after controlling for age, sex, and race, each one-standard-deviation increase in neuroticism score was associated with a 10% increase in mortality risk (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.09–1.11). While this association remained significant, it was partially explained by socioeconomic status, health behaviors, and chronic medical conditions \[8\]. Across all causes of death, neuroticism and loneliness demonstrated the strongest associations with mortality resulting from intentional self-harm, respiratory diseases, and digestive diseases\[8\].

**The Physiological Basis of the Big Five Personality Traits and Their Relationship with Aging**

**\[Hallmarks of Aging\]**

The "Hallmarks of Aging" represent the core biological mechanisms widely recognized by the scientific community as driving aging in humans and other mammals. Carlos López-Otín et al. (2013) first proposed nine hallmarks of aging in _Cell_\[9\]; as longevity research deepened, the team expanded this framework to 12 hallmarks of aging in 2023\[10\].

One of these hallmarks is chronic inflammation. In biogerontology and geriatric medicine, the persistent, low-grade systemic chronic inflammation that arises with age in the absence of overt infection is termed "inflammaging." An extensive body of research demonstrates that controlling systemic chronic inflammation not only influences lifespan, but serves as a pivotal determinant of healthspan.

Comparing centenarian offspring with age-matched controls, Arai et al. (2015) found that centenarian offspring exhibited significantly lower composite inflammation scores (difference: −0.795, 95% CI: −1.436 to −0.154). Furthermore, inflammation predicted functional capability (explaining 10.8% of variance) and cognitive capability (explaining 8.6% of variance) in (semi-)centenarians better than chronological age or sex\[11\].

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are commonly used as inflammatory markers. In single-sample studies, elevated neuroticism frequently co-occurs with higher levels of IL-6 and CRP. However, a meta-analysis by Luchetti et al. (2014) found no consistent association between neuroticism and either CRP or IL-6, noting substantial heterogeneity across studies\[12\].

In three nationwide samples spanning the entire adult lifespan (combined N = 26,305), Luchetti et al. (2014) showed that higher conscientiousness was associated with lower CRP levels. This association remained virtually unchanged after adjusting for smoking; while somewhat attenuated after adjusting for body mass index (BMI), it did not disappear. Compared to those in the highest quartile of conscientiousness, individuals in the lowest quartile had up to a ~50% increased risk of exceeding the clinical threshold for CRP; each standard-deviation increase in conscientiousness was associated with a 10%–15% reduction in this risk. Their meta-analysis of published literature further supported that conscientiousness is negatively correlated with both CRP (pooled estimate approximately −0.05) and IL-6\[12\].

Chronic stress is intricately linked to aging, acting upon several core mechanisms of senescence: chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial overload, telomere attrition, and the accelerated ticking of epigenetic clocks.

McEwen (1998) defined allostatic load (AL) as: "Allostasis is an essential component of maintaining homeostasis. When these adaptive systems are turned on and turned off again efficiently and not too frequently, the body is able to cope effectively with challenges that it might not otherwise survive. However, there are a number of circumstances in which allostatic systems may either be overstimulated or not perform normally, and this condition has been termed 'allostatic load' or the price of adaptation"\[13\].

Chronic stress is a primary driver of AL. Biomarkers of AL span the cardiovascular, metabolic, immune, and neuroendocrine systems. Yoneda et al. (2023) found a small but significant positive association between neuroticism and AL, a small but significant negative association between conscientiousness and AL, and a small negative association between openness and AL\[14\]. In addition, a four-year longitudinal analysis from the Health and Retirement Study revealed that during middle and older adulthood, cumulative physiological dysregulation was associated with accelerated declines in extraversion, conscientiousness, and agreeableness\[15\]. This suggests that physiological dysregulation can alter personality traits.

Chronic stress accelerates aging and drives allostatic load. Conversely, long-lived individuals possess a superior capacity to resist aging, which may grant them a heightened ability to sustain allostatic homeostasis and, consequently, a greater resilience to preserve their personality stability.

**\[Cortisol\]**

Cortisol is a glucocorticoid produced by the adrenal cortex. Due to its pivotal role in mounting stress responses, it is commonly referred to as the "stress hormone." Cortisol elevates blood pressure and blood glucose levels and exerts immunosuppressive effects.

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis secretes cortisol during stress responses, thereby influencing psychological and physiological adaptation. While cortisol measured via saliva, blood, or urine captures only transient fluctuations in HPA axis activity, hair cortisol provides an index of long-term, trait-stable systemic secretion. Erickson et al. (2021) assessed the Big Five personality traits at baseline in a cohort of 90 participants and monitored interpersonal goals pursued during prominent psychosocial stress events over the subsequent 4–5 weeks (totaling 1,949 entries). Hair samples were subsequently collected to evaluate cortisol secretion over the preceding two months. After controlling for the other four personality traits, neuroticism remained a significant positive predictor of hair cortisol concentration\[16\].

The NR3C1 gene encodes the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that mediates the biological actions of cortisol and other glucocorticoids. It is expressed across diverse tissues—including the brain (hippocampus, hypothalamus, and cerebral cortex), immune cells, liver, and adipose tissue—and is intimately involved in regulating stress responses, metabolism, inflammation, and immune function. Within the central nervous system, GRs in the hippocampus and hypothalamus govern negative feedback regulation of the HPA axis. Olczak et al. (2019) identified specific genetic variants (such as rs2963154, rs10515522, and rs291841) that are enriched among nonagenarians and centenarians\[17\]. Polymorphisms in the NR3C1 gene can alter receptor abundance or function, thereby modulating homeostatic regulation\[17\]. The fact that exceptionally long-lived individuals harbor specific NR3C1 variants provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for their lower emotional instability (neuroticism); higher NR3C1 expression confers a superior capacity to regulate cortisol dynamics.

Early-life stress increases DNA methylation at the NR3C1 promoter region in the hippocampus, suppressing glucocorticoid receptor expression and impairing negative feedback efficacy\[18\] —epigenetic alterations that can persist into adulthood.

Furthermore, Chubar et al. (2023) demonstrated that daily minor stressors interact with NR3C1 DNA methylation levels, such that elevated NR3C1 DNA methylation acts synergistically with daily hassles. Specifically, individuals with higher NR3C1 DNA methylation exhibited lower autonomic nervous system (ANS) stress adaptability, marked characteristically by blunted parasympathetic withdrawal. Importantly, the interactive effect of NR3C1 methylation and daily stressors on stress-system functionality was detectable as early as early adolescence\[19\].

**\[Dopamine\]**

Current research in personality neuroscience indicates that the dopaminergic system is linked to the Big Five personality traits, though this relationship is far from a simplistic "one neurotransmitter corresponds to one trait" framework. Among the traits, the association between extraversion and the dopamine system is the most prominent: dopamine mediates reward value coding, approach motivation, and reward learning, while individual sensitivity to rewarding and incentive cues is considered a key biological substrate of extraversion. Simultaneously, dopamine is implicated in novelty seeking, information pursuit, exploratory behavior, and cognitive flexibility, thereby suggesting a connection with openness to experience. By contrast, direct links between dopamine and neuroticism, agreeableness, or conscientiousness appear weaker or more complex, likely shaped by the interplay of other neurotransmitter systems, cognitive control mechanisms, and environmental factors.

A PET/SPECT meta-analysis encompassing 95 studies and 2,611 healthy adults by Karrer et al. (2017) demonstrated that dopamine D1/D2 receptor availability declines with age, averaging a loss of approximately 3.7% to 14% per decade\[20\]. Furthermore, a longitudinal study by Karalija et al. (2022) confirmed that D2 receptors decrease with age, yet with substantial inter-individual variability in the rate of decline\[21\]. A reasonable conjecture is that long-lived individuals exhibit a markedly slower rate of dopamine receptor attrition.

Monoamine oxidase is a critical enzyme tied to dopamine metabolism. Monoamine oxidase (MAO) is a flavin-containing enzyme localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane, categorized into two isozymes, MAO-A and MAO-B, which catalyze the oxidative deamination of monoamine neurotransmitters and dietary amines. Using quantitative immunoblotting, one study assessed the regional distribution (n = 38) and lifespan developmental/aging trajectories (ranging from 21 hours to 99 years) of both MAO isozymes in normal postmortem human brains. In infants, MAO-A abundance exceeded that of MAO-B; however, MAO-B levels rose more rapidly before age 1 and, unlike MAO-A, continued a steady, sustained increase throughout the lifespan into late senescence\[22\].

**\[Serotonin\]**

DeYoung’s personality neuroscience framework (2009) synthesizes the Big Five personality traits into two higher-order metatraits. "Stability"—composed of the shared variance among low neuroticism, high agreeableness, and high conscientiousness—is theoretically linked to serotonergic projections from the raphe nuclei that constrain negative emotionality, aggression, and impulsivity. "Plasticity"—comprising extraversion and openness—is more conventionally linked to dopaminergic systems governing reward and exploration. Accordingly, this framework posits that serotonergic functioning primarily underpins the Stability domain rather than being evenly distributed across all five personality dimensions\[23\].

Pharmacological challenge research (2019) provides conditional empirical support for this hypothesis. In a community sample (N = 441), Wright et al. administered intravenous citalopram, utilizing changes in serum prolactin as an index of central serotonergic responsivity. Neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and the overall Stability metatrait were all related to the prolactin response. However, these associations were nonlinear and contingent upon statistical modeling strategies: higher agreeableness, higher conscientiousness, and lower neuroticism each predicted a heightened response, an effect most pronounced at the extreme ends of the trait continuum\[24\].

**\[Oxytocin\]**

A study by Andari et al. (2012) demonstrated that plasma oxytocin levels were positively correlated with extraversion, while showing no significant correlations with neuroticism, openness, agreeableness, or conscientiousness. Concurrently, peripheral oxytocin levels were negatively correlated with gray matter volume in the right amygdala and right hippocampus—a region whose volume was likewise inversely related to extraversion. Based on these findings, the authors suggested that individual variations in oxytocin may be linked to affiliative personality traits through structural variation within emotion-regulation neural circuits\[25\].

In a cohort of 1,593 Han Chinese college students, Chong et al. (2019) observed that the rs2770378 variant of the oxytocin–neurophysin I gene (OXT) was associated with extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism, where AA homozygotes reported traits that foster positive social interactions. Other loci within this genetic region have also been linked to phenotypes such as spirituality, empathy, and neuroticism, with certain effects displaying sex-specific interactions; however, these findings warrant further replication in independent cohorts\[26\].

**The Big Five Personality Traits and Psychological Well-Being**

Personality traits are associated not only with physical health outcomes, but are also intimately linked to an individual’s subjective psychological state. A frequently examined question is whether exceptionally long-lived cohorts possess a greater propensity toward an optimistic mindset. Lee et al. (2019) tested this hypothesis utilizing data from two large prospective cohorts: a female cohort from the Nurses’ Health Study followed for approximately 10 years, and a male cohort from the Veterans Affairs Normative Aging Study followed for roughly 30 years. Their findings indicated that individuals in the highest quartile of optimism had an average lifespan 11% to 15% longer than those in the lowest quartile. The likelihood of surviving to age 85 was approximately 1.5 times greater for women and 1.7 times greater for men compared to the least optimistic group. These associations remained robust even after adjusting for educational attainment, chronic conditions, depressive symptoms, and lifestyle factors such as smoking, diet, and alcohol use\[27\].

As a manifestation of positive psychological traits, optimism overlaps with, but is not entirely synonymous with, the broader construct of Psychological Well-Being (PWB). Developed by Carol Ryff, the PWB framework emphasizes that mental health is not merely the absence of negative symptoms, but also involves positive psychological functioning. This model delineates six core dimensions: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth.

To what extent, then, do the Big Five personality traits associate with these dimensions of psychological well-being? A meta-analysis by Anglim et al. (2020), which synthesized 462 studies encompassing approximately 335,000 individuals, demonstrated that neuroticism displayed the strongest negative correlation with psychological well-being; extraversion showed a robust positive correlation; conscientiousness exhibited a moderately strong positive correlation; agreeableness demonstrated a moderate positive correlation; and openness also showed a positive correlation\[28\]. **The Big Five Personality Traits and Psychiatric Disorders**

Robust and replicable associations exist between the Big Five personality traits (neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness) and psychiatric disorders, though the magnitude of these associations and their diagnostic specificity vary across traits and diagnostic categories.

A meta-analysis by Kotov et al. (2010), synthesizing 175 studies and 851 effect sizes published between 1980 and 2007, showed that patients with depressive, anxiety, and substance use disorders consistently exhibited high neuroticism (mean Cohen's d = 1.65) and low conscientiousness (mean d = -1.01). Most disorders were also accompanied by low extraversion, with the most pronounced deficits observed in dysthymic disorder (d = -1.47) and social phobia (d = -1.31) \[30\].

Malouff et al. (2005) conducted a meta-analysis of 33 studies examining the relationships between the five factor dimensions and diagnostic/symptom scores across depression, bipolar disorder, phobic and panic disorders, alcohol use disorders, eating disorders, schizophrenia, and several somatoform and dissociative symptom scales. The pooled effect sizes across disorders revealed high neuroticism (Cohen's d = 0.92), low conscientiousness (d = -0.66), low extraversion (d = -0.41), and low agreeableness (d = -0.38), with openness near zero (d = 0.05). Among the four substantially correlated traits, neuroticism demonstrated the largest effect size, followed by conscientiousness\[31\].

A meta-analysis of 59 prospective studies encompassing 443,313 participants by Jeronimus et al. (2016) demonstrated that the unadjusted prospective associations of neuroticism with anxiety, depression, and non-specific psychological distress ranged from d = 0.50 to 0.70. After controlling for baseline symptoms and psychiatric history, these associations halved to d = 0.10 to 0.40; however, the adjusted effects showed virtually no decay over extended follow-up periods. Unadjusted prospective associations with substance abuse and thought disorders were weaker (d = 0.03 to 0.20). This indicates that high neuroticism signifies a pre-existing premorbid vulnerability configuration rather than mere state artifact or post-morbid psychological scarring\[32\].

In terms of personality disorders, a meta-analysis by Saulsman and Page (2004) indicated that most DSM-IV personality disorders share high neuroticism and low agreeableness, whereas the direction of extraversion diverges across disorders\[33\]. A facet-level meta-analysis by Samuel and Widiger (2008) largely corroborated theoretical expectations: borderline personality disorder was primarily characterized by high neuroticism (weighted r ≈ 0.54), low conscientiousness, and low agreeableness; avoidant personality disorder by high neuroticism and low extraversion; antisocial personality disorder by low agreeableness and low conscientiousness; obsessive-compulsive personality disorder stood out as one of the few categories positively correlated with high conscientiousness; and histrionic personality disorder was distinguished by elevated extraversion\[34\].⁠

In conclusion, high neuroticism is robustly linked to a wide spectrum of psychiatric disorders. Given that long-lived individuals characteristically exhibit low neuroticism, it is highly probable that they have a lower prevalence of psychiatric illness.

**Autism, the Big Five Personality Traits, Inflammation, and Psychiatric Disorders**

In a systematic review and partial meta-analysis examining temperament, character, and personality pathology in adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), Vuijk et al. (2018) found that ASD severity was positively correlated with neuroticism and negatively correlated with extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. Furthermore, it was positively associated with paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal, avoidant, and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders\[35\].

Earlier classic studies in the general population corroborate this exact profile. Austin (2005) investigated an undergraduate sample and observed that high scores on the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ) were associated with high neuroticism, low extraversion, and low agreeableness\[36\]. Wakabayashi et al. (2006) reported among Japanese university students that AQ was negatively correlated with extraversion and conscientiousness and positively correlated with neuroticism; individuals with high AQ scores exhibited a characteristic profile of high neuroticism, low extraversion, and low conscientiousness\[37\].

A meta-analysis across 98 studies covering 54 peripheral blood cytokines (encompassing 4,236 autistic individuals and 3,333 healthy controls) by Volk et al. (2026) demonstrated that levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-4, CXCL1, and MIF were significantly elevated in autistic individuals, mostly displaying small-to-moderate effect sizes. The authors noted that this pro-inflammatory cytokine profile primarily captures between-group differences between autistic individuals and neurotypical controls; within the autistic cohort itself, cytokine levels showed no significant association with the severity of autistic traits. Moreover, given that over one-third of the included studies carried a high risk of bias, the researchers reanalyzed the data after excluding these studies and further found that IL-7 and IL-1RA levels were also significantly higher in autistic individuals compared to controls\[38\].

Paralleling the high neuroticism seen in autism is an elevated vulnerability to psychiatric comorbidities. Michaelson et al. (2021) found that autistic individuals with an IQ ≥ 120 were nearly 15 times more likely to remain undiagnosed into adulthood compared to autistic peers with an IQ < 70. Among autistic individuals, self-reported feelings of incompetence correlated most strongly with increases in Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ); concurrently, self-, parent-, and teacher-reported anxiety all significantly increased with higher FSIQ (all p < 0.05)—a striking divergence from the protective, mitigating effect that FSIQ typically confers in non-autistic populations\[39\]. Casten et al. (2023) observed that autistic individuals with superior cognitive abilities frequently presented with severe internalizing symptoms and feelings of helplessness, and autistic children endorsing suicidal ideation exhibited significantly higher cognitive abilities. Phenotypically and genetically, elevated cognitive ability represents an unexpected risk factor for suicidal ideation in individuals diagnosed with or at risk for autism\[40\].

**The Big Five Personality Traits and Adult Attachment Styles**

Adult attachment theory originates from John Bowlby's framework, which posits that human infants possess an innate behavioral system directed toward seeking proximity and protection from caregivers. Repeated relational experiences are internalized as "internal working models" that predict both the availability of others and one's own worthiness of care. Hazan and Shaver proposed that adult romantic relationships functionally continue this behavioral system: romantic partners serve as primary attachment figures, individuals seek proximity under stress, and separation elicits distress, protest, or withdrawal.

Following factor analyses of existing self-report measures, Brennan, Clark, and Shaver identified that individual differences can be effectively conceptualized along two roughly orthogonal, continuous dimensions. _Attachment anxiety_ involves chronic concerns regarding rejection and abandonment, resulting in hyperactivating strategies such as compulsive reassurance-seeking and hypervigilance to relational cues. _Attachment avoidance_ involves discomfort with interpersonal closeness and emotional intimacy, resulting in deactivating strategies such as suppressing attachment needs, enforcing self-reliance, and maintaining emotional detachment. Individuals scoring low on both dimensions are classified as _secure_; high anxiety and low avoidance characterize the _anxious_ (preoccupied) style; low anxiety and high avoidance define the _dismissing-avoidant_ style; and high levels on both dimensions constitute the _fearful-avoidant_ style.

A review by Noftle et al. (2006) revealed that attachment anxiety exhibits a moderate-to-strong positive correlation with neuroticism, is virtually unrelated to openness, and demonstrates weak, inconsistent negative correlations with extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. Although both anxiety and neuroticism involve elevated sensitivity to negative emotionality, attachment anxiety specifically targets fears of relational abandonment, whereas neuroticism spans broader evaluative and emotional domains. The authors also found that attachment avoidance is weakly to moderately negatively correlated with extraversion and agreeableness, is generally unrelated or only weakly negatively related to openness, and displays inconsistent relationships with neuroticism and conscientiousness across studies. Secure attachment is associated with lower neuroticism and higher extraversion and agreeableness, showing only a weak positive correlation with conscientiousness and no significant relationship with openness\[41\].

Research by Sagone et al. (2023) investigating attachment styles and psychological well-being demonstrated that secure attachment is positively correlated across all dimensions of psychological well-being; by contrast, avoidant and anxious attachment are inversely correlated with psychological well-being \[42\]. Their data indicate that diminished psychological well-being is tied to elevated attachment anxiety and avoidance. Both dimensions foster psychological rigidity and blunt resilience and expressive awareness, thereby severely degrading overall well-being\[42\].

A systematic review by Sonfelianu et al. (2025) analyzing data across 12 studies (encompassing 91,078 females and 346 males) revealed that, compared to the general population, autistic adults are significantly more prone to developing insecure attachment and consistently register higher scores on both anxious and avoidant attachment dimensions\[43\].

Furthermore, an investigation by Ein-Dor et al. (2018) into epigenetic modifications of oxytocin and glucocorticoid receptor genes and avoidant attachment behavior in young adults revealed that avoidant attachment was significantly associated with heightened DNA methylation at the promoter regions of NR3C1 (and OXTR)\[44\].

As established earlier, long-lived cohorts characteristically exhibit lower neuroticism and higher extraversion. It is therefore reasonable to infer that exceptionally long-lived individuals are more likely to possess a secure attachment style. Conversely, the personality profile of fearful-avoidant individuals—characterized by high neuroticism, low extraversion, and low agreeableness—combines the three primary personality factors known to depress psychological well-being. It can thus be inferred that individuals with a fearful-avoidant attachment style experience the lowest psychological well-being among the four attachment styles.

Let us now examine the dimensions related to autism:

British clinical psychologist Simon Baron-Cohen proposed the Empathizing–Systemizing (E-S) theory to examine cognitive styles, classifying individuals based on their capacities for empathic and systemizing thinking. It employs the Empathizing Quotient (EQ) and the Systemizing Quotient (SQ) to assess these individual differences. Empathy represents the combined ability to identify another person’s mental states and respond with an appropriate affective reaction. Systemizing, on the other hand, targets inanimate physical environments and constructs that can be understood without emotional processing—such as mechanical, numerical, or abstract systems. Systemizing is the drive to analyze the underlying rules and regularities governing such systems in order to predict and control their behavior. Baron-Cohen et al. (2003) demonstrated that females scored significantly higher on average on the EQ (female mean = 47 vs. male mean = 38), whereas males scored higher on the SQ (male mean = 30 vs. female mean = 24). Furthermore, individuals with Asperger syndrome and high-functioning autism scored higher on the SQ than neurotypical controls (male mean = 36, female mean = 34) and had markedly lower EQ scores compared to controls (male mean = 18, female mean = 23)\[45\].

A cross-cultural study (2016) investigating samples from four countries—China, Germany, Spain, and the United States—revealed that across the total sample, EQ correlated with Agreeableness at r = 0.467, Conscientiousness at r = 0.408, Extraversion at r = 0.306, and Neuroticism at r = -0.149. In hierarchical regression analyses, Agreeableness alone accounted for approximately 18.5% of the variance in EQ\[46\].

**\[Luteolin\]**  
Luteolin is a natural flavonoid widely present in celery, broccoli, chrysanthemum, thyme, honeysuckle, and various botanical medicines. Renowned for its potent bioactivity, it possesses marked antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiallergic, antimicrobial, and neuroprotective properties. Clinically and nutraceutically, luteolin is historically used as an expectorant, antitussive, and therapeutic for respiratory inflammatory conditions. In recent years, its emerging potential in suppressing tumor progression, conferring cardioprotection, and preventing neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease has made it a focal point of natural product drug development.  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism.

**\[Farrerol\]**  
Farrerol is a natural dihydroflavonoid primarily derived from _Ericaceae_ species (notably the dried leaves of _Rhododendron dauricum_, known in traditional medicine as Manshanhong). It is well recognized for its prominent expectorant, antitussive, and antiasthmatic actions. Modern pharmacology demonstrates that farrerol also possesses potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, cerebrovascular/cardiovascular protective, hepatoprotective, and antitumor properties, mitigating tissue fibrosis and oxidative stress damage via multiple signaling pathways. Furthermore, farrerol acts as an enhancer of DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair.  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism.

**\[Forskolin\]**  
Forskolin is a natural labdane diterpene extracted from _Coleus forskohlii_. It bypasses membrane receptors to directly activate adenylyl cyclase (AC), significantly elevating intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels, which subsequently activates downstream protein kinase A (PKA) and hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL). It is widely sold as a dietary supplement and serves as a core constituent in chemical reprogramming formulations.  
As noted earlier, neuroticism is closely tied to cortisol dynamics. Studies indicate that activation of the cAMP signaling cascade upregulates _NR3C1_ mRNA and protein levels \[47\]; theoretically, the cAMP activator forskolin can ameliorate or rescue stress-induced cortisol dysregulation. Furthermore, Roszczyk et al. (2014) showed that forskolin stimulates oxytocin and vasopressin release from the rat hypothalamo-neurohypophysial system, a process inhibited by melatonin \[48\].  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism, increased Extraversion, increased Agreeableness.

**\[Tranylcypromine\]**  
Tranylcypromine is a classic amphetamine-derivative, irreversible, non-selective monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI). Its primary mechanism involves the irreversible inhibition of both MAO-A and MAO-B, blocking the enzymatic degradation of key central monoamines—serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine—thereby significantly elevating synaptic neurotransmitter concentrations to produce potent antidepressant and anxiolytic effects. Simultaneously, tranylcypromine acts as a lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) inhibitor, establishing it as an epigenetic modulator of great interest in oncology and a core component in chemical reprogramming regimens.  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism, increased Extraversion, increased Openness, increased Agreeableness, increased Conscientiousness.

**\[Sodium Valproate\]**  
Sodium valproate is a widely utilized, classic first-line broad-spectrum antiepileptic drug and mood stabilizer. Its pharmacology is multi-targeted: on one hand, it inhibits GABA transaminase to reduce the degradation of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), markedly raising inhibitory neurotransmitter concentrations in the brain; on the other hand, it blocks voltage-gated sodium channels and low-threshold T-type calcium channels, suppressing aberrant high-frequency neuronal discharge. Additionally, it functions as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor to modulate synaptic plasticity and gene transcription, making it another core pillar of chemical reprogramming.  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism, increased Agreeableness.

**\[CHIR99021\]**  
CHIR99021 is a highly potent, selective small-molecule glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3α/β) inhibitor and one of the essential tool compounds in stem cell biology and regenerative medicine. By competitively binding ATP to potently inhibit GSK-3 kinase activity, it blocks the phosphorylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation of β-catenin, thereby promoting β-catenin cytosolic accumulation and nuclear translocation to robustly activate canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling. It serves as an indispensable component of chemical reprogramming.  
GSK-3 promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IFN-γ) and chemokines while suppressing anti-inflammatory cytokines (notably IL-10 and IL-1 receptor antagonists). Inhibiting GSK-3 lowers pro-inflammatory cytokines and elevates IL-10.

_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism.

**\[Repsox\]**  
Repsox is a potent, selective small-molecule inhibitor of the transforming growth factor-beta type I receptor (ALK5/TGFBR1), serving as a benchmark compound in stem cell and epigenetic reprogramming. Mechanistically, it competitively blocks ALK5 kinase activity, abrogating downstream Smad2/3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to disrupt classical pro-differentiation and pro-fibrotic TGF-β/Smad signaling cascades. It is a core component of chemical reprogramming.  
_Putative impact:_ Decreased Neuroticism.

**\[Pirfenidone\]**  
Pirfenidone is an orally active small-molecule pyridone with broad-spectrum antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory properties, and the first-in-class core therapy approved globally for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Its multi-target mechanism centers on inhibiting TGF-β1 expression and signaling, blocking aberrant fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation, suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-1β), and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS).
